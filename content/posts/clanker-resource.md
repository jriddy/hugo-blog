In case you've been living under a rock for the past few months, the internet has taken to calling all chatbots, robots, and all other attempts at non-human autonomy _clankers_, as a slur.  As a vehicle of expressing hate, it's got a lot going for it.  It has not one but two /k/ sounds, which we all know is the funniest sound.  It reminds you of broken devices driving you insane with their incessant noise.  It's easy to say in anger, with the nasaly central vowel able to carry a lot of venom as you enunciate the word.  _Goddamn clankers again_, you can imagine saying, with all the vitriol you can muster.

It's not the only anti-robot slur to come out of this rising tide of _robophobia_ on the internet.  I'm not going to list them all here, because some veer too close to slurs for certain types of humans, but I will mention _tinskin_ and _cogsucker_ as personal favorites, although the latter seems more appropriate for someone who is overly fond of robots.

To say anti-AI sentiment is high would be an understatement.  Folks are exhausted of having half-baked generative AI shoved in their faces at every turn.  Nobody wants to talk to Samantha, your AI assistant.  No one has ever wanted an AI washing machine.  No normal, well-adjusted human being with friends, family, hobbies, a functioning moral compass, and a favorite flavor of ice cream can hear the word _agentic_ and take it seriously.  Which makes it confusing and infuriating that there's a whole class of people who think all of this is a great idea.

Yet instead of directing our frustration that those people, it seems easier to direct the hate at the _bots_.  Robophobia is definitely funnier, in post-ironic sort of way.  It's edgy in that it co-opts shocking and hateful tactics from _actual racism_ but directs it at an acceptable target.  It's ironic in that you can say that you're only using the hate language in gest, but post-ironic in that you really do hate the bots.  But it's still misdirected.

The bots are not alive.  The AI is not taking our jobs.  This current generation of technology will not do what its most ardent promoters claim it will do, and we have little to worry from the technology itself.  What we should fear is the executive-investor class that has so gleefully embraced the notion of _ending human labor as we know it_.

## Software

If you're not a software developer, it might surprise you to know that very few of us are currently worried about being automated out of a job.  That's not to say there's not a lot of _anxiety_ in the industry: layoffs are becoming more common, job pressures are increasing, conditions are getting worse, and rewards are harder to come by, all while the hiring process—once the career-growth fast track for developers—has become a sort of cold war between employers and the employees they ostensibly want to hire.  That's all very disconcerting, but it's not an existential threat to the very nature of our role.

This is because software development is, like, maybe _25%_ coding.  It's hard to put an actual percentage on it because it varies so much by positions, but I can say for sure that almost no one spends 100% of their time with hands on keyboards cranking out code.  And this rate actually tends to go _down_ with experience.  As you take on team leadership and staff or principal roles in an org, you spend far more time writing and mentoring and analyzing and strategizing than you do writing actual _computer code_.  Even for less senior roles, you'll spend a huge chunk of your time thinking about _what_ needs to be coded and _how_ best to do it.

For developers that have fully embraced LLM assistants, they are spending more time _writing_ prose to give the right context to the models to allow them to do their jobs.  The models are incapable of the kinds of analysis needed to produce meaningful results, so effective users of this kind of coding end up writing out that analysis into plain language so the machine will have a chance of implementing the code correctly.  Then they need to test and review that code and judge how it will fit alongside other bits in the system.  Then they need to be able to troubleshoot problems and make the kinds of precision changes that LLMs will never excel at.

The glaring exception to this is junior developers, who have traditionally spend _more_ time coding than their more experienced developers, as they are often implementing tasks that come pre-analyzed and specified from a more senior team member.  And it's true that junior devs are having a harder time getting a job.  While it's tempting to think that this is because more senior programmers have embraced the vibes to the point where juniors are no longer needed, there's a decent amount of evidence to suggest that GenAI has not made programmers much more effective, and it's entirely possible any productivity gains are almost entirely offset by reduced quality and security of the resulting code.

But that's hard to prove conclusively, and "AI is taking junior devs' jobs!" is a compelling narrative when we can see AI adoption and junior dev unemployment increasing at the same time.  Cue the line: _correlation is not causation_.  It's very easy to argue that the software industry as a whole overhired in the early years of this decade, and the slow hiring has to do with broader macroeconomic trends like the end of ZIRP-era spendthriftiness and a slowing economy held out of recession almost entirely by a tech hype bubble.  Hiring juniors is about investing in the future, and most higher-ups are going to be more reluctant to do that when the only certainty you have about tomorrow is that your executive and investor bosses are going to be demanding that you squeeze more blood from stone.

It's that squeeze, really, that is driving all of this.  Executives squeeze because they themselves are _squozen_.  They need to show results, and GenAI is just a well-timed trend that provides a convincing cover story for cost-cutting.  In the past (and in the present too!), it has been offshoring jobs, importing cheaper labor, or just plain, old-fashioned high-pressure management.  Let's lay off half the team and expect everyone to do twice as much.  Don't like it?  We'll find a fresh-off-the-boat Indian making half your salary to do your job.  This is definitely still happening, but GenAI has the allure of being perceived part of an inevitable future, and you can be seen as innovative for deploying AI-first strategies.

There are plenty of fields that are more directly affected by these trends.  I talk about software because that's what I know, but it's not even the hardest-hit segment.  Entry-level work of all kinds is getting hard to find.  "Soft" tech and tech-adjacent jobs are under serious pressure.  I know design teams that have been reduced by 75%, and whose higher-ups expect that Figma's AI features can make up for the difference.  Not surprisingly, what really happens is that the few remaining folks end up generating derivative and hurried slop in effort to keep their jobs.  And after a year or so of grinding it out under this regime, burnout is rampant the the few remaining members are beginning to quit.

The obvious problem with these plans is that the work suffers.  Greatly.  Some time ago, ~~usurious predator~~ buy-now-pay-later provider Klarna fired all all its customer service staff only to [reverse course 2 years later](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/business/klarna-ceo-sebastian-siemiatkowski-ai-job-cuts-hiring-b2755580.html), citing reduced quality of service, after their stock dipped precipitously.   Of course, to avoid the unpleasantries of having to pay folks a living wage for their labor, they are experimenting with an Uber-style gig work model.

## Storytellers

What's clear in examples like this is that executives tend to have a hard time gauging the impact of knowledge work on their business.[^1]  They simply do not have the right information available to them to understand these things in detail.  Nor do they have the kinds of knobs and levers at hand that could affect meaningful change in business outcomes if they _did_ have that information.  But they are constantly under the gun to produce results that investors like.  This combination of intense pressure and lack of fine control is enough to drive anyone insane.[^2]

We all know this right?  I mean, we can't always articulate it well, but we _know it in our bones_ that the more power you have, the more you have to answer for.  This, along with the perception that management is mostly _politics_, serves to keep a lot of folks from even trying to climb the ladder.  We don't want to want to be responsible for the kinds of things managers and executives are responsible for, especially since we won't have direct control over most factors that could drive those outcomes.  But this thing we fear—being expected to _answer_ for what happened—is precisely the root of an executive's power.  Those who are _expected_ to give answers are the ones who _get_ to give answers.  This is the power of _narrative_.

You could be forgiven for assuming that executives derive their power from things like budgets, headcounts, project mandates, and the criteria by which successes are measured.  These are important details, for sure, but they are all part of the narrative.  Almost any change in those parameters can be justified if you can craft and sell the right story around it.  Need more money and people to do a project?  You need to create a narrative around how you will use that money to make more money in the future, expressed in terms that are amenable to the other decision makers in your organization: satisfactory ROI estimates, a nod to the CEO's pet interest, financing via a shift from OpEx to CapEx, and your own track record of landing projects.  And each of these things is its own sub-narrative, supported by meta-narratives.  Think of all the guesswork and assumptions that necessarily go into providing an ROI estimate; think of all the assumptions that go into making .  Or think of an executive's track record: this is very _literally_ a narrative.  How can anyone know how responsible someone at their level was for the successes of their previous projects?  How do we know they're not just lucky?  We don't.  We have to rely on the various stories told about this.

Businesses are complex things.  If you've ever wondered why it seems like higher-ups at your job can seem like they don't even know what's going on, it's because _they don't even know what's going on_.  It is often legitimately hard to know what's going on at a business of any scale at all because there is too much to do.  This is why we hire people and create org charts with lines of reporting: so we can farm out the details of what is going on to other people.  This organizational model relies on _relevant_ information being communicated up the chain, but there are a thousand forces that interfere with that being done correctly, not the least of which is the difficulty of determining _relevance_.

It's this kind of complexity that allows _narratives_ to thrive.  When things are simple, you don't need much narrative to explain it.

The world of narrative references other narratives, some of which may bear limited resemblance to any kind of ground reality.  All that really matters is that other storytellers accept your narratives.  This is the great limit on the power of narrative: you have to get other people to believe you.  Other storytellers can and will challenge your numbers or your track record and put you on the defensive because narratives that spring entirely from your purview are susceptible to your manipulation and everyone knows it.  Fortunately the business world is constantly abuzz with widely-accepted narratives about what is popular and useful and desirable.  Leaning into one of these is usually safe because anyone challenging these narratives has to challenge the enormous number of storytellers that repeat them.

The importance and inevitability of a GenAI transformation is an obvious example of this kind of narrative...

These narratives are driven by an even higher-level meta-narrative: growth.  The Growth Narrative is the idea that all businesses should be eternal engines of economic growth, perpetually increasing shareholder value at all costs.  Ostensibly they should do this by providing some useful value to customers, but that regarded as more of an inconvenient necessity than a desirable goal.  What matters is that the stock price goes up.  Everything else is details.  This is "line go up" as the moral philosophy of an entire economy, what Ed Zitron coined [the Rot Economy](https://www.wheresyoured.at/the-rot-economy/).

So, with the pressures, powers, and weaknesses of an executive made clear, it's easy to see how they are mainly incentivized to create convincing narratives about progress and growth.  They just need preside over the production of enough financial metrics to support those narratives that they can dismiss or suppress any dissenting ones.

## Jo example, potential throwaway, repurpose

Let's make this a bit more concrete.  We all know someone at work who really _holds things down_.  I'm talking the employee that calmly steps in and and fixes things when shit hits the fan.  Let's call her Jo.  Jo prevents the accident or incident from occurring.  Jo finds the critical fix in the dead of night that keeps everything working.  Jo saves the big customer contract with her heroics.   Jo may be well known within her area of expertise, but it's unlikely that she is super well-known outside her department.  Let's imagine Jo has an autistic son who is just starting high school and is having a really hard time.  Now all of a sudden all that energy and mental focus she brought to holding down the fort at work is going towards her son.  Of course, Jo doesn't want to share this relevant information; she's worried it will be used to confirm longstanding prejudices about women in the workplace and make her look week.  Jo's boss sees the reduced performance; he doesn't want to report it because he doesn't want it known that he built such an imbalanced team.  Jo's boss's boss notices that incidents are taking longer to resolve and key customers are unhappy; she isn't able to attribute a reason why, because she doesn't understand who is critical and who isn't.

Ideas:

- Robophobia is funny in a post-ironic way, but it misses the point.
- Generative AI is not replacing workers en masse in most fields.
- Macroeconomic forces and stories are affecting this
- But it does reveal the mindset of decision makers in business and public life, who are champing at the bit to get rid of the pesky inconvenience of employing people
- The executive-investor class has a reductive and myopic view of knowledge work
- I am not a Marxist, but this is definitely a class issue, not a technology one
- Even if the reaction is a bit misdirected, its positive that people see this stuff as a threat, and not as some herald of a bright future
- Neoluddism is a reasonable response, but smashing the bots will not save us
- We need to lose our societal patience for technology overlords.  Stop buying corporate lies.  Start organizing against this.
- We need a slur for the executive-investor class instead

[^1]: I talk a lot about _knowledge work_ in this article, but I'd argue this applies to nearly all work not performed on an assembly line.  Knowledge work differs from skilled trade work a bit in that its artifacts tend to be a bit more abstract, but any work that demands the judicious application of skills to diverse tasks is bound to be hard to understand from a distance.

[^2]: I cannot speak to the actual mental health of all executives, for obvious reasons.  I assume that some folks manage to make it there and remain or become well-adjusted human beings.  But my experiences suggest that power definitely takes its toll, and it's far worse on those that go into it [clear-eyed](https://www.ribbonfarm.com/2013/05/16/the-gervais-principle-vi-children-of-an-absent-god/) than those who [stumble into their roles](https://www.ribbonfarm.com/2010/04/14/the-gervais-principle-iii-the-curse-of-development/).

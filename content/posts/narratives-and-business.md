What's clear in examples like this is that executives tend to have a hard time gauging the impact of knowledge work on their business.[^1]  They simply do not have the right information available to them to understand these things in detail.  Nor do they have the kinds of knobs and levers at hand that could affect meaningful change in business outcomes if they _did_ have that information.  But they are constantly under the gun to produce results that investors like.  This combination of intense pressure and lack of fine control is enough to drive anyone insane.[^2]

We all know this right?  I mean, we can't always articulate it well, but we _know it in our bones_ that the more power you have, the more you have to answer for.  This, along with the perception that management is mostly _politics_, serves to keep a lot of folks from even trying to climb the ladder.  We don't want to want to be responsible for the kinds of things managers and executives are responsible for, especially since we won't have direct control over most factors that could drive those outcomes.  But this thing we fear—being expected to _answer_ for what happened—is precisely the root of an executive's power.  Those who are _expected_ to give answers are the ones who _get_ to give answers.  This is the power of _narrative_.

You could be forgiven for assuming that executives derive their power from things like budgets, headcounts, project mandates, and the criteria by which successes are measured.  These are important details, for sure, but they are all part of the narrative.  Almost any change in those parameters can be justified if you can craft and sell the right story around it.  Need more money and people to do a project?  You need to create a narrative around how you will use that money to make more money in the future, expressed in terms that are amenable to the other decision makers in your organization: satisfactory ROI estimates, a nod to the CEO's pet interest, financing via a shift from OpEx to CapEx, and your own track record of landing projects.  And each of these things is its own sub-narrative, supported by meta-narratives.  Think of all the guesswork and assumptions that necessarily go into providing an ROI estimate; think of all the assumptions that go into making .  Or think of an executive's track record: this is very _literally_ a narrative.  How can anyone know how responsible someone at their level was for the successes of their previous projects?  How do we know they're not just lucky?  We don't.  We have to rely on the various stories told about this.

Businesses are complex things.  If you've ever wondered why it seems like higher-ups at your job can seem like they don't even know what's going on, it's because _they don't even know what's going on_.  It is often legitimately hard to know what's going on at a business of any scale at all because there is too much to do.  This is why we hire people and create org charts with lines of reporting: so we can farm out the details of what is going on to other people.  This organizational model relies on _relevant_ information being communicated up the chain, but there are a thousand forces that interfere with that being done correctly, not the least of which is the difficulty of determining _relevance_.

It's this kind of complexity that allows _narratives_ to thrive.  When things are simple, you don't need much narrative to explain it.

The world of narrative references other narratives, some of which may bear limited resemblance to any kind of ground reality.  All that really matters is that other storytellers accept your narratives.  This is the great limit on the power of narrative: you have to get other people to believe you.  Other storytellers can and will challenge your numbers or your track record and put you on the defensive because narratives that spring entirely from your purview are susceptible to your manipulation and everyone knows it.  Fortunately the business world is constantly abuzz with widely-accepted narratives about what is popular and useful and desirable.  Leaning into one of these is usually safe because anyone challenging these narratives has to challenge the enormous number of storytellers that repeat them.

The importance and inevitability of a GenAI transformation is an obvious example of this kind of narrative...

These narratives are driven by an even higher-level meta-narrative: growth.  The Growth Narrative is the idea that all businesses should be eternal engines of economic growth, perpetually increasing shareholder value at all costs.  Ostensibly they should do this by providing some useful value to customers, but that regarded as more of an inconvenient necessity than a desirable goal.  What matters is that the stock price goes up.  Everything else is details.  This is "line go up" as the moral philosophy of an entire economy, what Ed Zitron coined [the Rot Economy](https://www.wheresyoured.at/the-rot-economy/).

So, with the pressures, powers, and weaknesses of an executive made clear, it's easy to see how they are mainly incentivized to create convincing narratives about progress and growth.  They just need preside over the production of enough financial metrics to support those narratives that they can dismiss or suppress any dissenting ones.

## Jo example, potential throwaway, repurpose

Let's make this a bit more concrete.  We all know someone at work who really _holds things down_.  I'm talking the employee that calmly steps in and and fixes things when shit hits the fan.  Let's call her Jo.  Jo prevents the accident or incident from occurring.  Jo finds the critical fix in the dead of night that keeps everything working.  Jo saves the big customer contract with her heroics.   Jo may be well known within her area of expertise, but it's unlikely that she is super well-known outside her department.  Let's imagine Jo has an autistic son who is just starting high school and is having a really hard time.  Now all of a sudden all that energy and mental focus she brought to holding down the fort at work is going towards her son.  Of course, Jo doesn't want to share this relevant information; she's worried it will be used to confirm longstanding prejudices about women in the workplace and make her look week.  Jo's boss sees the reduced performance; he doesn't want to report it because he doesn't want it known that he built such an imbalanced team.  Jo's boss's boss notices that incidents are taking longer to resolve and key customers are unhappy; she isn't able to attribute a reason why, because she doesn't understand who is critical and who isn't.

[^1]: I talk a lot about _knowledge work_ in this article, but I'd argue this applies to nearly all work not performed on an assembly line.  Knowledge work differs from skilled trade work a bit in that its artifacts tend to be a bit more abstract, but any work that demands the judicious application of skills to diverse tasks is bound to be hard to understand from a distance.

[^2]: I cannot speak to the actual mental health of all executives, for obvious reasons.  I assume that some folks manage to make it there and remain or become well-adjusted human beings.  But my experiences suggest that power definitely takes its toll, and it's far worse on those that go into it [clear-eyed](https://www.ribbonfarm.com/2013/05/16/the-gervais-principle-vi-children-of-an-absent-god/) than those who [stumble into their roles](https://www.ribbonfarm.com/2010/04/14/the-gervais-principle-iii-the-curse-of-development/).
